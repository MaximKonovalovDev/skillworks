# Cheatsheet — James, Psychology: Briefer Course (1892; Gutenberg #55262)

One-page recall. Page numbers are the 1892 Holt edition pagination given in
the book's own table of contents.

1. **Habit is second nature (Ch. X, p. 134).** "Habit a second nature!
   Habit is ten times nature" (Wellington). Habit is "the enormous
   fly-wheel of society, its most precious conservative agent" — the
   discharged veteran who drops his dinner at the shouted word
   "Attention!", the menagerie-tiger that crept back into its broken
   cage after the railroad accident. By thirty, character "has set like
   plaster."

2. **Consciousness flows, never recurs (Ch. XI, p. 151).** "Within each
   personal consciousness, thought is sensibly continuous" — without
   breach, crack, or division. Hence "the stream of thought": the same
   state of mind is never had twice, and permanently recurring ideas are
   a fiction. Every object comes with a "fringe" of relations.

3. **The self is Me and I (Ch. XII, p. 176).** The Me is material, social,
   and spiritual; the I is the "pure ego," the passing Thinker. Rivalry
   of the Mes, their hierarchy, mutations of the self, alternating
   personalities and mediumships all hang on this split.

4. **Attention is narrow and will rides it (Ch. XIII, p. 217; Ch. XXVI,
   p. 448).** To keep attention an object must change; genius is sustained
   attention. Closing move of the Will chapter: "What holds attention
   determines action" and "volitional effort is effort of attention."

5. **Association runs on habit and interest (Ch. XVI, p. 253).** "The
   ultimate cause of association is habit." Recall is governed by
   frequency, recency, vividness, and emotional congruity; focalized
   recall is "association by similarity." Total recall is rare — which is
   why Ribot's foreshortening (Ch. XVIII, p. 300) is a condition of
   memory's health: "one condition of remembering is that we should
   forget."

6. **Emotion follows the body (Ch. XXIV, p. 373).** "The feeling, in the
   coarser emotions, results from the bodily expression": the bodily
   manifestations must first be interposed — "we feel sorry because we
   cry, angry because we strike, afraid because we tremble," not the
   reverse. Without the bodily states the perception would be "pale,
   colorless, destitute of emotional warmth."

7. **Will is ideo-motor plus decision (Ch. XXVI, p. 415).** Ideo-motor
   action: movement following "unhesitatingly and immediately" on the mere
   idea of it (the pin on the floor, nuts after dinner). After
   deliberation come five chief types of decision; unhealthy wills split
   into the explosive will (defective inhibition, exaggerated impulsion)
   and the obstructed will. The free-will question is "insoluble on
   strictly psychologic grounds."
