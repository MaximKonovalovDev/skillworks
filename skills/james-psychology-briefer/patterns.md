# Patterns — James, Psychology: Briefer Course (1892; Gutenberg #55262)

Reusable moves, each named, each with its chapter anchor.

## 1. The Fly-Wheel (Ch. X, Habit, p. 134)

**Move:** turn a chosen action into a pathway in the centres so it runs
without attention. Concatenated acts need guiding sensations only while
learning (the pianist's book held under the arm drops at first, never
later); then "processes of inattentive feeling" carry them.
**Use it:** drill the opening of any performance until it is "second
nature"; pedagogical maxims on habit-formation (p. 142) say launch new
habits strongly, never suffer exceptions, and seize the first chance to
act on a resolution.
**Watch for:** the fly-wheel cuts both ways — by thirty the character
"has set like plaster," and society's conservatism is habit's doing.

## 2. The Stream (Ch. XI, The Stream of Consciousness, p. 151)

**Move:** treat thinking as a continuous flow with substantive
(resting-place) and transitive (flight) parts, every object fringed with
relations, consciousness "especially interested in some one part of its
object."
**Use it:** when analyzing a decision or memory, look at the transitive
flights between the named conclusions, not just the conclusions — the
"topic" of thought lives there.
**Watch for:** no state recurs; "permanently recurring ideas are a
fiction," so introspection never steps in the same stream twice.

## 3. The Interposed Body (Ch. XXIV, Emotion, p. 373)

**Move:** reverse the common-sense order — bodily changes follow the
perception directly, and feeling those changes *is* the emotion. "We feel
sorry because we cry, angry because we strike, afraid because we
tremble"; the bodily manifestations must first be interposed.
**Use it:** to change the subtler emotions (Ch. XXIV, p. 384), work
through expression and action rather than arguing with the feeling; the
corollary is verified in the text (p. 382).
**Watch for:** the claim covers the coarser emotions; without the bodily
states perception stays "pale, colorless, destitute of emotional warmth."

## 4. Attention-Is-Will (Ch. XIII, Attention, p. 217; Ch. XXVI, Will, p. 448)

**Move:** will is "a relation between the mind and its 'ideas'":
whatever idea possesses attention discharges its motor effects "as a
matter of course" — in instinct, emotion, ideo-motor action, hypnotic
suggestion, and the fiat of all five types of decision.
**Use it:** want an act? Keep its idea steadily occupying
consciousness ("volitional effort is effort of attention," p. 450);
want restraint? Keep the contrary reasons present — their "bare presence
to the mind" gives the veto.
**Watch for:** diagnose failure as explosive will (defective inhibition,
p. 437; exaggerated impulsion, p. 439) versus obstructed will (p. 441)
before prescribing.

## 5. Forgetting-as-Health (Ch. XVI, Association, p. 253; Ch. XVIII, Memory, p. 287)

**Move:** retention plus recall both ride "paths formed by habit in the
brain"; a good memory needs persistence *and* numerousness of paths. But
total recall is rare, and Ribot's foreshortening — the omission of "an
enormous number of the facts" — is what makes remembering possible at
all: "If we remembered everything, we should on most occasions be as ill
off as if we remembered nothing."
**Use it:** cramming (p. 295) fails because native retentiveness is
unchangeable (p. 296); improve memory by multiplying paths (association,
order, interest), not by effort of retention.
**Watch for:** recognition (p. 299) and pathological double
personalities (p. 301; Janet's anæsthesia experiments) show memory
coming and going with sensibility.
