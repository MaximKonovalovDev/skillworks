# Glossary — James, Psychology: Briefer Course (1892; Gutenberg #55262)

- **Habit (Ch. X, p. 134).** A pathway formed in the nerve-centres by
  repeated action, making performances secondarily automatic — "second
  nature," indeed "ten times nature." The basis of concatenated acts and
  the "enormous fly-wheel of society."

- **Stream of thought (Ch. XI, p. 151).** James's name for thinking as
  "sensibly continuous" personal consciousness — without breach, crack,
  or division — with substantive (resting) and transitive (flight)
  parts, each object fringed with relations. No state of mind is ever
  had twice.

- **Me / I (Ch. XII, p. 176).** The Me is the self as known — material
  (body, possessions), social (recognition by others), spiritual
  (inner faculties); the I, or "pure ego," is the passing Thinker that
  knows them. Personal identity is explained by identity of function in
  successive passing thoughts.

- **Attention (Ch. XIII, p. 217).** The narrow field of consciousness:
  dispersed or focal, voluntary or involuntary, momentary unless its
  object changes. Genius is sustained attention; its physiological
  conditions are adaptation of the sense-organ and arousal of the idea
  of the object. Bears directly on free-will (p. 237).

- **Association by similarity (Ch. XVI, p. 253).** Focalized recall in
  which the law of interest (frequency, recency, vividness, emotional
  congruity) selects what a cue revives. James's summary: the ultimate
  cause of association is habit; similarity is no elementary law.

- **Foreshortening (Ch. XVIII, p. 287).** Ribot's term for the omission
  of "an enormous number of the facts" from recollected time. "One
  condition of remembering is that we should forget"; oblivion is no
  malady of memory but a condition of its health and life.

- **Ideo-motor action (Ch. XXVI, p. 432).** Movement that follows
  "unhesitatingly and immediately" upon the mere idea of it, with no
  express fiat — brushing dust from a sleeve mid-conversation, taking
  nuts after dinner. "Simply the normal process stripped of disguise";
  its condition is the absence of any conflicting notion.

- **Explosive / obstructed will (Ch. XXVI, pp. 437–441).** Two
  unhealthinesses of will: the explosive will, from defective inhibition
  or exaggerated impulsion, discharges too readily; the obstructed will
  (the exhausted sailor, the freezing-morning bed) cannot discharge at
  all. Health is the steady occupancy of consciousness by the motive
  idea — "volitional effort is effort of attention" (p. 450).
